Your lidar cannot see what you can fall into

A defence ground vehicle's lidar detects 92.9% of what sticks up and 11.5% of what it can fall into — at full budget, every ray, forty times over. One geometric test closes that gap at a twentieth of the points.

01 — The finding

Ditches are not a budget problem. They are a blindness problem.

Negative obstacles — trenches, craters, washouts — are detected by the absence of laser returns. A narrow trench is not entered by beams. It is stepped over.

Coverage inside each planted feature, measured from 40 full scans — every ray, forty times, no budget limit at all:

FeatureKindObserved
Boulderpositive96.2%
Boulderpositive90.9%
Trench, 2.4 m widenegative59.6%
Trench, 3.0 m widenegative40.6%
Trench, 16 m wide, 6 m deepnegative1.5%
Driven area, for reference—83.6%

Spending more rays cannot fix this. A full scan already spends everything.

And it gets worse with more data

The share of real hazards reported as safe to drive over rises from 1.9% at a 2% budget to 6.4% at full scan. The system sees the trench rim, judges it flat, and calls it drivable — while the interior is never observed. More data buys more confidence, not more sight.

02 — The mechanism

Bumps degrade linearly. Ditches degrade quadratically.

bump: h ≥ r · Δθ but ditch: w ≥ r² · Δθ / hsensor

Measured against a level plane with real Ouster OS1-64 geometry — Δθ = 0.714°, 1.8 m mast. The closed form understates the real spacing by about 40% at 40 m, so these are the measured numbers, not the formula's:

RangeBeam spacing on the ground Smallest bumpSmallest ditch
10 m0.72 m0.12 m0.69 m
20 m3.51 m0.25 m2.77 m
30 m9.17 m0.37 m6.23 m
40 m16.51 m0.50 m11.08 m

At 30 m a stock OS1-64 can drive past a six-metre-wide ditch and register nothing. Not a degraded reading — nothing.

03 — The consequence

The same geometry sets a speed limit nobody published

A 1 m ditch is guaranteed straddled only out to 12 metres. With a 2 m/s² braking model, that caps safe speed at 6.4 m/s.

The capability statement

A stock OS1-64 on a UGV that must never drive into a one-metre ditch cannot safely exceed 23 km/h — and the sensor gives no indication that this is so.

This holds whether or not anything we built works. It is a property of hardware already in the field, derived from geometry.

04 — The detector

The quadratic that causes the blindness, normalised, detects it

Two adjacent beams that both return land a predictable distance apart on level ground. If the far one lands much further out, the surface between them dropped away.

anomaly = measured gap ÷ predicted gap at that range

The comparison must be local and range-aware. A fixed threshold flags flat ground several times over, because ground spacing grows quadratically.

Threshold: chosen by sweep, not by eye

Scored against the identical terrain with the ditches removed — the only honest control.

3.0 is the knee. Against 2.0 it costs six points of recall and removes 89% of the false flags. Going further costs another five points for far less.

The headline

91% of negative obstacles at 5% of the point budget. A full scan with no absence reasoning finds 12%. Twenty times fewer points, seven times better detection — and it costs 43 ms a frame, against 500 ms for the approach it replaces.

05 — The demo

Same sensor, same budget, opposite verdicts

Both systems at a 2% budget — 1,280 rays against 1,256, so ours uses slightly fewer. Approaching a 3 m wide, 2.2 m deep trench.

FrameDistance to ditch ConventionalRaysense
418.4 m unknown ahead DITCH AHEAD
614.8 m unknown ahead DITCH AHEAD
811.4 m clear to drive DITCH AHEAD
108.6 m DITCH AHEAD DITCH AHEAD

We warn at 18.4 m. The conventional system reports the ground as clear to drive at 11.4 m and only warns at 8.6 m — 9.8 metres later, and inside the braking distance it would need at any real speed.

It does not hold, either: at frames 20 and 22 the conventional system reverts to unknown ahead, because it reasons about heights it can see rather than gaps it cannot.

06 — The allocator

It wins one metric and loses the other. Both are true.

Whole-map recall asks did you eventually map every ditch, including one 60 m off your route? Coverage wins that, so plain decimation wins it — ours loses, 74.9% against 57.6% at a 2% budget.

Warning distance asks how far off was the ditch ahead when you noticed it? That is what a vehicle cares about, and concentration wins it:

Budgetuniform front wedgeRaysense
2%12.3 m19.1 m 23.2 m
5%15.0 m21.5 m 26.4 m
10%18.3 m30.2 m 28.3 m
20%21.9 m30.2 m 28.3 m

At a 2% budget the allocator gives 1.88× the warning of uniform — 5.8 seconds to react instead of 3.1 — and it is the only method that finds all four ditches at that budget. Above about 10% the static front wedge overtakes it. If a slide quotes an allocator number, quote the 2% one, and say the limitation before being asked.

07 — What we got wrong

Three things measurement overturned

Each of these was believed, written down, and then disproved by building it. Volunteering them is the most credible thing we can do in front of a technical jury.

We believedMeasurement said
The contribution is adaptive allocation — same safety at a third of the points The contribution is a detector. Allocation loses to plain decimation on recall and wins only on warning distance, at tight budgets
The safety floor is Δθ ≤ h/r Ditches bind quadratically. A floor sized for bumps is met while ditches stay invisible
A ditch shows the literature's rear-wall signature A ditch is usually stepped over. The absence test finds almost none; the gap test finds 91%

Standing limitations

  • 73% precision, not 100%. Crest occlusions produce genuine range gaps. Whether that is a false positive is arguable — the ground behind a crest is unobserved — but it is not zero.
  • Nothing has touched real sensor data. RELLIS-3D is the intended corroboration; the sensor's azimuth resolution, label packing and pose frame are all still unverified, and the first is the denominator of every budget percentage here.
  • The official problem-statement text has never been read. All of this is built from the title and the domain.
