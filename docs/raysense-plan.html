01 — Thesis

The insight the whole project rests on

This is what separates us from every other team and from every existing paper. Everything after this is execution.

Negative obstacles — ditches, craters, washouts, shell holes — are detected by the absence of laser returns. You see a hole because the ground stopped returning where the ground plane said it should.

Adaptive sampling deliberately creates absences. It skips regions on purpose.

Put those two facts together and a fatal ambiguity appears.

ObservationExplanation AExplanation BExplanation C
No return from this patch of ground A ditch Occlusion shadow We never looked

On a road that ambiguity is a nuisance. On a UGV crossing broken terrain at speed, it is how you lose the vehicle.

Why nobody has solved this

All published adaptive-lidar work targets urban autonomous driving — KITTI, nuScenes, lanes, cars, pedestrians — where negative obstacles are essentially absent. Meanwhile the entire negative-obstacle literature assumes dense uniform scanning. The two fields have never been put in the same room.

Raysense puts them in the same room. Our allocator carries an explicit three-valued state — OBSERVED UNKNOWN CANDIDATE_NEGATIVE — never conflates unknown with empty, and actively spends measurement budget to resolve dangerous ambiguity.

That is a defensible research contribution, a safety argument a defence audience grasps instantly, and it hands us the most persuasive demo available: watch the naive method drive into a ditch, then watch ours refuse to.

02 — Architecture

What we build

A point-budget allocator. Given a budget of B measurements per frame, where B is far smaller than a full scan, decide how to distribute them across the field of view to maximise terrain-map quality and obstacle safety.

The per-frame loop

Single pass. No sparse-then-rescan round trip — on a moving platform the world has already shifted by the time you go back.

  1. PredictWarp the map from frame t−1 into frame t using ego-motion. Propagate object tracks. Age every cell.
  2. Build the need mapScore every angular bin: elevation gradient, staleness, predicted moving-object footprints, frontier cells, proximity to the braking corridor — and unresolved negative-obstacle candidates.
  3. Apply the safety floorA hard geometric constraint, allocated before anything adaptive. Never traded away.
  4. Allocate the remainderDistribute the rest in proportion to need, with a per-bin cap so it cannot collapse onto one region.
  5. AcquireThrough one of three interchangeable sensor backends — this is our answer to “which lidar?”
  6. Update the mapFuse the selected points into a quadtree elevation map, subdividing where variance or gradient is high.
  7. DisambiguateTrack rays, not just points. A cell is EMPTY only if a ray actually traversed it and returned from beyond. Everything else stays UNKNOWN.
  8. Output traversabilityThree-valued: traversable, blocked, unknown. Unknown is never treated as safe.

The safety floor — provable, not heuristic

To guarantee a beam strikes an obstacle of height h at range r, the angular sample spacing must satisfy:

Δθ ≤ h / r therefore hmin(r) = r · Δθ

Inside the braking corridor we enforce Δθ ≤ h_target / d_brake and allocate that budget first. This falls straight out of honest geometry — and it hands us a capability no other team will have: the allocator can state the maximum safe speed for a given point budget and sensor.

Stack

03 — Evidence

How we prove it

The experiment is the project. Design it first, build backwards from it.

Data — two legs, deliberately

RELLIS-3D is the primary. A real all-terrain UGV in rugged off-road terrain, Ouster OS1, 13,556 point-wise annotated scans, 20 semantic classes — the closest public dataset to a DRDO ground-vehicle scenario that exists. The full recorded scan is our ground truth; our algorithm chooses which points it would have acquired. That is how we escape the obvious kill shot: “of course it works, you wrote the simulator and the answers.”

Simulation is the second leg, for closed-loop behaviour on a moving platform and — crucially — for deliberately planted negative obstacles with exact ground truth, which real datasets barely contain.

Say that two-leg rationale out loud. It turns “why simulation?” from an attack into evidence that we thought about experimental design.

Baselines — all at identical point budget

  1. Full scan (100%) — the quality ceiling
  2. Uniform decimation — every k-th beam. The honest naive baseline.
  3. Random subsampling
  4. Static front-focused ROI — the “reasonable engineer” baseline, and the one we must beat convincingly, because a judge will propose it

The two charts that win

One — task metric against point budget. Ours dominating every baseline, converging on full-scan performance somewhere near 25–35% of the points.

Two — the failure case. Uniform decimation at 30% budget misses a ditch. Ours catches it. Same frame, same budget, side by side.

The number we are chasing

“Matched obstacle-detection recall at 3× fewer points and 2.4× lower per-frame latency.” A ratio, a task metric, a named platform. Never “significantly fewer.”

05 — Scope

What we deliberately do not build

This goes on a slide. Volunteering your boundaries reads as confidence and kills the gotcha before it is asked.

  • SLAM / localisation. We consume ego-motion — RELLIS-3D ships GPS/IMU, simulation gives it free. Estimating it is a solved, separate problem.
  • Sensor fusion. Lidar only. Adding cameras would confound the exact measurement we are making.
  • Path planning. We define the braking corridor. We do not plan routes.
  • Dashboards, cloud, accounts. Nobody asked for them.
  • Hardware. This is a software problem statement.

Each is a deliberate decision with a reason attached. Always say the reason.

07 — Presentation

Winner mentality

Nine rules. Worth more than any feature we could add in the same time.

  1. Open in their language, not ours.The first thirty seconds should sound like a capability briefing, not a college project. “UGVs in unstructured terrain operate under a hard SWaP budget…” — never “Hi, we're Raysense and we made a cool lidar thing.”
  2. Preempt the novelty question yourself.Do not wait to be caught. “Adaptive lidar sampling is established — AEye ships it, NEC published it in 2020, there's a 2025 paper on temporal cues. What does not exist is…” This flips our biggest risk into a credibility asset. Technical juries relax visibly when a team knows its own field.
  3. Lead with the failure, not the success.Show naive sampling missing the ditch first. Let them feel it. Then show ours. Almost no team structures a pitch this way, and it is the most persuasive order available.
  4. One number, repeated three times.Slide 1, the demo, the closing line. Judges see twenty teams and retain exactly one number per team. Choose it deliberately.
  5. Treat rounds 1–3 as free consulting.The finale has four stages; the first three are mentoring and critique, the fourth is the power round. Ask each juror: “What would you need to see to trust this in the field?” Write it down where they can see you writing it. Then ship it and open the next round with “you asked for X — here it is.” Most teams waste these rounds defending their original design.
  6. Never say “we invented.”Say characterised, constrained, quantified, made deployable. Overclaiming is the fastest way to lose a technical jury.
  7. Bring offline fallbacks.Recorded demo video, seeded local data, zero network dependency, spare laptop, spare cable. Teams lose rounds to laptops, not to logic.
  8. One presenter — but everyone answers for their own module.Juries probe by asking the quiet member a question. Every person must be able to defend their component unprompted.
  9. Volunteer the limitations before you're asked.“Here's what we don't handle, and here's why that's the right scope.” Confidence, not weakness.
