The three-move answer

Judges ask short questions. Teams ramble. Three sentences, in this order, every time.

Move 1
The claim
“Ninety-one percent, at five percent of the points.”
Move 2
The evidence
“Forty frames, ground truth known, committed CSV.”
Move 3
The bound
“On our testbed — we have not run real sensor data yet.”

Move 3 is not weakness. Move 3 is why they believe moves 1 and 2. A team that volunteers its own limit has told the judge it knows where the limit is. A team that doesn't has told the judge it never looked.

When you don't know, say exactly this and then stop: “We haven't measured that. What we did measure is ⟨nearest thing⟩. To answer properly we'd need to ⟨the specific experiment⟩.” Never invent a number — one fabricated figure discounts every other figure you gave.

✕“It should work on real data” → say: “We have not run real data. Zero frames.”
✕“Our AI…” → there is no learning in this system. Say geometry.
✕“Basically”, “kind of”, “roughly” → reads as hedging under pressure
✕“Good question!” → spends 1.5 of your 25 seconds
✕Talking over a judge → if they interrupt, stop mid-word and listen
✕Two of you answering one question → it reads as the first answer being wrong
