Why more rays cannot fix it

A bump scales with range. A ditch scales with range squared.

Two adjacent beams land a predictable distance apart on level ground, and that distance grows as r²·Δθ / h. A ditch narrower than the gap is straddled by both beams and returns nothing anomalous. A full scan already spends every ray, so spending more cannot help. A bump is different — it intercepts a beam as soon as its height reaches r·Δθ. Linear, not quadratic.

The number nobody publishes

That geometry is also a speed limit.

The sensor gives no indication that this is so. You can compute it for any lidar and any platform from four numbers, and it is actionable before a line of our software ships.

The contribution

The same quadratic, normalised, becomes the detector.

If two adjacent beams both return, they should land a predictable distance apart. If the far one lands much further out than predicted, the surface between them dropped away. Dividing by the prediction makes the test range-independent — one threshold works at 10 m and at 40 m.

anomaly = measured gap ÷ predicted gap
prior method range-normalised gap test

What we got wrong

The most useful thing we did was disprove our own idea.

disprovedOur headline idea lost.

We set out to show that smart ray allocation was the win. We built three allocators and measured them. On whole-map recall, plain uniform decimation beat all of them — 91.4% against 79.3% at a 5% budget. The real contribution turned out to be the detector, which needs no allocator at all. We changed the claim to match the measurement instead of the other way round.

correctedA number on our own slide was wrong by 29×.

We had been quoting "43 ms per frame". Building a proper benchmark showed it was never written to a CSV and did not reproduce — it was a full-scan cost quoted next to a 5%-budget result. The real figure is 1.5 ms. The error understated us, and we published the correction rather than quietly swapping the number.

withdrawnWe claimed zero false positives. We were wrong.

We had measured it on a single azimuth column. Across a full scan, rolling terrain produced 70 flags. We withdrew the claim, then found out what those flags actually were: crest occlusions — the ground rises, the far beam clears it, and the shadow behind reads exactly like a hole. A crest is reached uphill and a ditch is not; the two populations don't overlap (0.039 against 0.069). That one test took false flags from 436 cells to 5.

standingPrecision is 79%, and that is a choice.

We can reach 87.5% — the levers are in the code. Every setting that gets there moves the share of real ditch cells we'd call drivable off 0.00%. On a system whose whole claim is that it never turns "I did not look there" into "safe to drive", that column outranks the precision column. We ship the honest row and commit the curve.

openZero frames of real sensor data. So far.

Everything above is a controlled testbed where ground truth is known rather than inferred — deliberately, because public off-road datasets contain almost no labelled ditches, so scoring against them means scoring against the sensor's own blind spot. RELLIS-3D corroboration is the next task, and until it runs we quote no result from it.
